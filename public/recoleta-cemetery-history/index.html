The History of Recoleta Cemetery: From a Friars' Orchard to a City of the Dead

Two hundred years in one walled block of Buenos Aires: a convent garden, a republican reform, an epidemic, a mayor with Parisian ambitions, and the families who turned a burial ground into a cemetery the whole world comes to see.

By EugeneUpdated November 20268-minute read
Buy the audio guide Tickets & hours Book a guided tour

Recoleta Cemetery looks as if it had always been there: a walled city of marble on five and a half hectares, 4,600 vaults in paved lanes, a graveyard that draws visitors from every continent. In fact almost nothing you see is older than 1881, the cemetery itself is younger than the republic that fills it, and it began as a kitchen garden behind a church on the edge of town. This is the story of how the garden became the city of the dead — and what each chapter left behind for you to see.

The people in the vaults — presidents, a caretaker, a girl in an Art Nouveau doorway — have their own stories, and this page deliberately leaves most of them alone: they belong to the TouringBee audio guide, 22 stops on an offline map, told at the doors where they happened. What follows is the history of the place itself.

Before the cemetery: friars on the edge of town

In the early 1700s the land north of the small colonial port was fields, river bank and a few farms. A community of Franciscan Recollects — recoletos, friars who withdrew from the city for a stricter life — settled there precisely because it was far from everything, and by 1732 they had finished their church, Nuestra Señora del Pilar, white, plain and colonial, which still stands beside the cemetery gate and is free to enter. Behind the convent lay the friars' orchard and vegetable garden. The neighbourhood took its name from them, and two centuries later so would the cemetery.

1822: the first public cemetery

Independence changed where the dead could lie. Colonial Buenos Aires buried its people in and around churches; the new republican government of Buenos Aires province, under governor Martín Rodríguez and his reforming minister Bernardino Rivadavia, wanted burials moved out of the city for reasons of hygiene and out of the Church's hands for reasons of politics. The Recoletos were dissolved as part of Rivadavia's ecclesiastical reform, and their orchard was the obvious place. On 17 November 1822 the Cementerio del Norte opened there as the city's first public cemetery. The French engineer Próspero Catelin, who also gave the Metropolitan Cathedral its temple front, laid out the ground; by 1828, under governor Manuel Dorrego, the cemetery had reached the perimeter it keeps today.

The early cemetery looked nothing like the marble city of today: grass, modest markers, an adobe wall, an iron gate. And its first occupants were not presidents. By the City's own account the first two burials were Juan Benito, a freed Black child, and María Dolores Maciel, a young woman. Recoleta's reputation as the cemetery of the elite came later; it is worth remembering that it began as a public ground for everyone.

1863: the year the Church lost the cemetery

For forty years the cemetery was public in name but Catholic in practice: the Church decided who could be buried in consecrated ground. In 1863 that broke. A Freemason died having refused the last rites; the bishop refused him burial; the family went over the bishop's head to President Bartolomé Mitre, and the quarrel ended with the cemetery secularised — a municipal institution open to the dead of any creed or none. It is the one moment when Freemasonry really did change Recoleta, which is why we resist reading Masonic signs into every compass and ladder on the doors (see our symbols decoder). How the quarrel actually played out, letter by letter, is told at the basilica door, stop 2 of the audio route; the political consequences are what matter here. From 1863 on, a tomb in Recoleta was a statement about family and nation, not about faith.

1871: an epidemic redraws the city

In the summer of 1871 yellow fever killed thousands in the crowded southern districts of Buenos Aires — San Telmo, Monserrat — and the families who could afford to leave moved north, to higher ground and cleaner air, to the quarter around the Recoletos' old church. The epidemic also overwhelmed the Cementerio del Norte: the city opened a new ground far to the west that same year, the cemetery that became Chacarita, with a “death train” carrying coffins out of the centre. The two cemeteries have divided the city ever since: Chacarita vast and democratic, Recoleta small and exclusive. The elite had moved in next door, and it would be buried next door too.

1881: a mayor builds a city for the dead

Torcuato de Alvear, who ran the municipal commission of a newly federalised Buenos Aires and in 1883 became its first mayor (intendente), wanted a capital that looked like Paris: boulevards, parks, façades. He gave the cemetery the same treatment. In 1881 the architect Juan Antonio Buschiazzo, Italian-born and prolific, rebuilt it as a miniature city — a Doric portico on Junín with its Latin inscriptions and carved allegories, a perimeter wall, straight paved avenues, blocks of family plots facing the lanes like town houses, a central crossing where a white Christ now stands. Everything you navigate by today dates from this plan. The portico is the clearest piece of it; our architecture article reads its symbols one by one.

The redesign also gave the cemetery its staff and its rules. One of the caretakers who worked here from 1881 until 1910 spent his life trying to afford a plot of his own among the families he served, and became a legend in the process — his statue, broom in hand, is a stop on the audio route, and the story is better heard there than read here.

1890–1930: the competition in marble

Argentina's export boom made Buenos Aires one of the richest cities in the world, and the families who ran it — landowners, bankers, generals, presidents — built in Recoleta the way they built on Avenida Alvear: with imported Carrara marble, French and Italian sculptors, bronze doors, stained glass, domes. A plot by the main avenue could cost as much as a city-centre apartment. The styles followed European fashion in sequence, Neoclassical giving way to Gothic revival, Art Nouveau around 1900, Art Deco by the 1930s, and a few families looked further, to Egypt and to pre-Hispanic America. Almost every vault you will photograph dates from these forty years, and so does the cemetery's international reputation: the obvious comparison with Père Lachaise in Paris was already being made a century ago.

The twentieth century: from private ground to heritage

By the 1940s the republic began to treat its cemetery as history. A national commission started cataloguing the country's heritage in 1940, and the first Recoleta tombs were declared national historic monuments in 1945; a single decree of 1946 listed 53 of them at once. The additions have continued by ones and twos ever since — more than ninety vaults today — and in 2007 the entrance gate itself was listed. In 1949 the Cementerio del Norte was officially renamed Cementerio de la Recoleta, the name everyone had used for a century.

And in October 1976, in a side lane, the twentieth century's most famous burial took place almost without ceremony: Eva Perón's body, after 24 years of exile, theft and hiding, was placed in the Duarte family vault under layers of concrete. That journey has an article of its own. From then on the cemetery had two kinds of visitor — the families who had always come, and the world.

Today: a working cemetery and an open-air museum

Recoleta is still a municipal cemetery, run by the City of Buenos Aires, with funerals and family visits every week; the City counts some 350,000 burials in two hundred years. It is also one of the city's main attractions, with an entry ticket for non-residents since 2022, a daily programme of official tours in Spanish, and, since 2003, a restoration campaign whose date is set into the portico floor beside 1822 and 1881. International lists have been kind to it — the BBC named it among the world's best cemeteries in 2011, CNN among the ten most beautiful in 2013 — and the cats that once lived here by the dozen have mostly been rehomed by volunteers. The lanes you walk are Buschiazzo's; the dead you look for are mostly Belle Époque; the institution is Rivadavia's. All three layers are visible at once, which is what makes the place worth reading rather than just photographing.

From my own visit

What history looks like on the ground is maintenance. Walking the lanes at nine in the morning, in the low light that comes over the wall from Junín, I kept seeing the same contrast within a few metres: a vault restored to the stone, bronze polished, fresh flowers behind the glass — and next to it one with a broken window, a spiral staircase going down into the dark and coffins on the level below, a family that ended or moved away or stopped paying. The cemetery is not a monument that was finished in 1881. It is two hundred years of families editing the same five hectares, and the editing is still going on.

A word about where you are

Because it is still in use, the rules are simple: give way to funerals and to families at a vault, don't touch doors or step inside open ones, and keep your voice down in the side lanes. Argentines come here to visit their dead, not to be part of your photograph.

The takeaway

A friars' garden in 1732, a public cemetery in 1822, a civil one in 1863, the elite's own after 1871, a city in miniature from 1881, a heritage site since 1945 and a place of pilgrimage since 1976. Walk in knowing those seven dates and the lanes stop being a maze of beautiful stone and become what they are: the biography of a city, told in marble by the people who ran it.

Frequently asked questions

When was Recoleta Cemetery founded?

It opened on 17 November 1822 as the Cementerio del Norte, the first public cemetery of Buenos Aires, on the former orchard of the Recoletos friars. The layout was by the French engineer Próspero Catelin; the decision came from governor Martín Rodríguez and his minister Bernardino Rivadavia.

Why is it called Recoleta?

After the Franciscan Recollect friars (recoletos) who settled on what was then the edge of the city in the early 18th century and built the church of Our Lady of the Pillar, finished in 1732. The neighbourhood took their name, and in 1949 the cemetery officially did too.

Who was the first person buried in Recoleta Cemetery?

By the City's own account, the first two burials were Juan Benito, a freed Black child, and María Dolores Maciel, a young woman. The cemetery of presidents began with two people nobody remembers.

Why did the Church lose control of the cemetery?

Because of a dispute in 1863 over the burial of a Freemason who had refused the last rites. The family appealed to President Mitre; the result was that the cemetery became a civil, municipal institution. The full story is told at the basilica door on the TouringBee audio route.

Is Recoleta Cemetery still in use?

Yes. It is an active municipal cemetery with funerals and family visits every week; more than ninety of its vaults are national historic monuments, and the entrance gate was listed in 2007. Visitors give way to mourners.

Why is Evita buried in Recoleta and not somewhere grander?

Because the Duarte family vault was there. After a 24-year journey her body was placed in it in 1976, in a side lane, under heavy concrete. Our Evita article tells the whole story; the audio guide tells it at the vault.
