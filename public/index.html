Why Visit Recoleta Cemetery?

EvitaLegendsFunerary artHistory

Recoleta is not a cemetery with a few famous graves. It is a city inside a city: 4,600 vaults and mausoleums on five and a half hectares, laid out in streets and blocks, with corner plots, domes, stained glass and marble angels — and the most visited grave in Argentina hidden in a side lane, marked only with the name “Duarte”.

Presidents, generals, Nobel laureates, an Irish admiral, a champion boxer, a caretaker who saved for decades to be buried among them, and a girl whose legend says she woke up inside her coffin. That is the cast. The setting is the most elegant neighbourhood of Buenos Aires, where the city decided in the 1880s to look like Paris and then built its dead a Paris of their own. This guide walks you through what Recoleta is, why it looks the way it does, when to come, how much it costs, and how to see the best of it without a group — the TouringBee audio tour does the storytelling on the spot.

The Story of Recoleta Cemetery

From a Friars' Orchard to the First Public Cemetery

The name comes from the Recollect Franciscans — los Recoletos — who settled on what was then the edge of Buenos Aires in the early 1700s and built the Basílica del Pilar, still standing next door. In 1822 a reforming government dissolved the order and turned the friars' garden into the city's first public cemetery. Burial had just been banned inside churches; this was the modern, civic alternative. The first people laid to rest here, a city chronicle records, were a child of freed slaves and a woman called María Dolores Maciel. The exclusive necropolis came later.

1871: Yellow Fever Moves the Rich North

In 1871 yellow fever tore through the crowded southern districts of Buenos Aires. Whoever could afford it fled north to the high ground of Recoleta — and never went back. The neighbourhood became the address of the elite, and its cemetery became their final one. Argentina was on its way to being one of the richest countries on earth; the French still say riche comme un Argentin. Families competed in marble the way they competed in mansions. A plot with a vault could cost as much as an apartment in the centre.

1881: A City for the Dead, With Streets and Corners

The Recoleta you walk today is largely the work of one decade. In 1881 the mayor, Torcuato de Alvear, ordered a full redesign, and architect Juan Antonio Buschiazzo gave the cemetery its neoclassical portico, its paved avenues and its blocks of family vaults facing the lanes like town houses. Read the gate before you go in: Requiescant in pace above the columns, an owl, an hourglass with wings, a snake biting its tail. The whole cemetery is written on that door.

Eva Perón's Longest Journey

Evita died of cancer in 1952 at 33, and the country wept in the streets. Her embalmed body was meant for a monument; instead, after the 1955 coup, it vanished. For sixteen years it was hidden, moved between offices and attics, and finally buried under a false name in Milan. Juan Perón recovered it in Madrid in 1971. It came home to Argentina in 1974 and, in 1976, was placed in the Duarte family vault at Recoleta — several metres down, behind heavy plates, in one of the most guarded graves in the world. The vault itself is modest. The flowers, the notes and the crowds are the monument. How to find Evita's tomb →

The Legends: a Girl at a Door, a Bride and Her Dog, a Caretaker's Dream

Every cemetery has stories; Recoleta's are unusually good, and the sculpture keeps them alive. Rufina Cambaceres died in 1902 on her nineteenth birthday; her Art Nouveau tomb shows her with one hand on the door, and the city has told the story of her waking inside the coffin ever since. Liliana Crociati was killed by an avalanche in Austria in 1970; her mother designed a neo-Gothic chapel with a bronze Liliana outside it, her dog Sabú at her feet, his nose polished gold by visitors' hands. David Alleno, a caretaker, saved for years to buy his own plot among the presidents and had himself carved with his keys, broom and watering can. We tell you which parts are documented and which are legend — the truth is usually more interesting.

Funerary Art: Four Styles on One Street

Stand at a crossing and you can see neoclassical columns, a neo-Gothic spire, an Art Nouveau woman with flowing hair and an Art Deco slab in a single glance. Italian sculptors worked here — Giulio Monteverde carved the chapel's crucifix, Victor de Pol put a condor on top of President Sarmiento's obelisk — and so did Lola Mora, Argentina's most famous sculptor. Look for the Egyptian-flavoured Art Deco vaults and for the doors: the hourglass, the inverted torch, the wreath, the butterfly. Each one is a sentence about the family inside.

Planning Your Visit

How Long You Need

Forty-five minutes covers the essentials: the gate, the central Christ, Sarmiento, Evita, Rufina. Ninety minutes is the sweet spot for most people. Give it two to three hours if you like reading doors and comparing styles. The lanes look alike, and a ten-minute crossing easily becomes forty once a bronze face catches your eye — which is exactly why an offline map helps.

Best Time to Come

Be at the gate at 9:00. The cemetery is empty, the light is low and warm on the marble, and you'll have Evita's lane to yourself. By 10:30 the coach groups arrive and the narrow passages jam. The last hour before 17:00 is the second-best window. Remember the seasons are reversed: December to February is high summer, hot and humid on the stone; June to August is mild. Best time to visit →

Tickets and the Entry Fee

Foreign visitors pay an entry fee; Argentine residents don't. Buy online on the City's Entradas BA site or at the gate with a card — the peso price changes with inflation, so check the official figure the week you travel. Free guided walks in Spanish leave the gate every hour. Full ticket guide →

Where to Stay

Recoleta itself is the classic choice: quiet, walkable, five minutes from the cemetery and the cafés of Plaza Francia. Palermo is livelier and a short taxi away. Check hotels in Recoleta on Booking.com →

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Recoleta Cemetery free to visit?

Not for foreign visitors. Since 2022 the City of Buenos Aires charges non-residents an entry fee (around ARS 24,000 in 2026, paid by card or QR). Argentine residents enter free with their ID. Tickets are sold online on the official Entradas BA site and at the gate.

What are the opening hours?

9:00 to 17:00, every day of the year. Free guided walks in Spanish leave the main gate every hour — from 10:00 on weekdays and from 9:00 at weekends and holidays.

How do I find Eva Perón's tomb?

She lies in the Duarte family vault — look for “Familia Duarte”, not “Perón” — in a narrow side lane a few minutes from the entrance. Our Evita article has step-by-step directions; the audio guide's offline map takes you straight there.

How long does a visit take?

Around 45 minutes for the essentials, 90 minutes for a proper walk, up to 2–3 hours if you love architecture and stories. The audio tour's 22 stops take about an hour and a half at a relaxed pace.

Is the audio guide available offline?

Yes. Download it once in the TouringBee app and it works fully offline — useful, because the tall marble lanes block mobile signal.

What languages is the audio guide in?

English and Spanish. This website is available in eight languages, but the audio narration for this tour is recorded in English and Spanish.

Does it work on iPhone and Android?

Yes, the TouringBee app runs on both iOS and Android.

How do I get to Recoleta Cemetery?

Subte line H to Las Heras, then a 10-minute walk; or a colectivo to Avenida Las Heras y Junín. Taxis, Uber and Cabify drop you at Junín 1760, right by the gate. From the centre it's a pleasant 30–40 minute walk.

Is it an active cemetery?

Yes. Families still visit, funerals still take place, and caretakers work in the lanes every day. Keep your voice down, don't touch doors or sculptures, and step aside for mourners.
