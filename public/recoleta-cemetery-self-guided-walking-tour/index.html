Can you see Recoleta Cemetery on your own? Completely. It's open 9:00–17:00 every day, the entry ticket for non-residents costs about as much as a lunch, residents walk in free, and nobody will stop you wandering the 4,600 vaults for as long as you like. The free route on this site even tells you where to turn. What you won't get is the stories: the signs inside are minimal, the free tours are in Spanish and on a schedule, and the paid ones move at a group's pace.

That's the gap this page is about. Without a guide, Recoleta is a beautiful set — angels, domes, a girl in marble at a door — and you walk past the best things in it without knowing. The TouringBee audio guide fixes that without taking away the freedom: 22 stories on an offline map, in walking order from the portico to the far lanes, which you start when you reach each tomb and pause when you want to look. One story, told to the end: Rufina Cambaceres

Stop 8 of the route, in a side lane off the main axis: a Carrara marble tomb in pure Art Nouveau, irises climbing the frame, and a life-size girl standing at a half-open door with one hand on the handle, as if about to step out. Her name is above her: Rufina Cambaceres. Here is what is documented. She was born on 31 May 1883, the daughter of the novelist Eugenio Cambaceres, one of the richest men in Argentina, and an Italian dancer, Luisa Bacichi. She died on 31 May 1902 — her nineteenth birthday — in the family home. Three doctors certified a cardiac cause, and she was buried the next day. Her mother later commissioned this tomb from the sculptor Richard Aigner.

And here is the legend — and it is a legend, told in Buenos Aires for a century without a single contemporary document behind it. That Rufina was not dead but in a cataleptic coma. That the night after the burial a caretaker heard noises from the vault, found the coffin shifted and the lid scratched from the inside. That she had woken in the dark, fought to get out, and died of the horror of it. That her mother, who could not forgive herself, had her carved for ever at the door, reaching for the handle. Some versions add that she was dressing for an evening at the Teatro Colón — which is impossible: the old theatre had closed in 1888 and the new one would not open until 1908.

Look at the sculpture and you understand why the story has never died. A girl touching a door looks like someone about to open it. Art doesn't prove the legend; it keeps it alive. The two rival explanations of why she collapsed that evening — and the famous name one of them involves — are in the audio at the tomb. There are 22 stories like this on the route: a caretaker who bought his place among the presidents, a boxer who knocked the world champion out of the ring, a body that disappeared for sixteen years. The route: 7 stages, 22 stories

Below are the seven main stages of the walk. In the app the route is broken into 22 audio stories with additional stops and illustrations; this is the main line, not every lane.

  1. The portico and the PantheonDoric columns, Requiescant in pace, and straight ahead the first and grandest avenue.
  2. The great avenuesPresidents and generals in a row: Sarmiento's condor, Mitre's Art Deco, the central crucifix where the lanes meet.
  3. Admiral Brown and the foundersA green column for an Irishman, a girl who married at fifteen against her parents, a doctor who fought the epidemics.
  4. Liliana and RufinaTwo young women outside their tombs: one in bronze with her dog, one in marble with her hand on the door.
  5. Eva PerónThe Duarte vault in a narrow side lane — black granite, bronze plaques, roses in the grille.
  6. The caretaker and the boxerDavid Alleno with his broom; Luis Ángel Firpo, the Wild Bull of the Pampas, life-size in his robe.
  7. The far lanesNobel laureates, Victoria Ocampo, a president who ended the dictatorship, and the “Argentine Nostradamus” to finish.

Did you know? Recoleta has no graves in the ground: it's a city of family vaults, most of them two or three levels deep, and the coffins you sometimes glimpse through a glass door are on the stairs down. The route passes several.

Useful to know before the walk

  • Start outside the portico on Junín 1760 — or anywhere else: the stops are on the map and you can begin at Evita if she's your priority.
  • Ground is paved but old and uneven, with narrow lanes and occasional steps; flat shoes. No café, no water inside.
  • Heat: in summer (December–February) the marble radiates it by midday. Go at 9:00 — emptier, cooler, better light. See when to go.
  • Tickets are not included: non-residents buy entry at the gate or on Entradas BA; residents free. Card or QR, not cash.
  • Getting there: Subte line H to Las Heras, ten minutes on foot; details in how to get there. Afterwards, the basilica, La Biela and the Bellas Artes museum are all within five minutes.
  • Respect: funerals and family visits take priority; lower your voice, don't touch doors or sculptures, don't feed the cats.

Frequently asked questions

Can I visit Recoleta Cemetery on my own, without a tour?

Yes. It's fully open to independent visitors from 9:00 to 17:00 every day; you buy the entry ticket (non-residents) at the gate or on Entradas BA and walk in. The only thing you don't get is the stories — the signage inside is minimal. That is what the audio guide adds.

How many stories are there and how long does the tour take?

22 audio stories on an offline map. At a relaxed pace the whole route takes about an hour and a half; you can do the essentials in 45 minutes or spend three hours — nothing is timed.

Do I need internet inside the cemetery?

No. Download the tour on Wi-Fi before you go; the map, the stories and the illustrations then work offline. Mobile signal between the marble walls is unreliable, so this matters.

Is the cemetery entry ticket included?

No. The audio guide is €9.99 for the tour only. Non-residents pay the cemetery's entry fee separately (about ARS 24,000 in 2026, at the gate or online on Entradas BA); residents of Argentina enter free with ID.

Do I have to start at the beginning of the route?

No. The stops are on the map and each story starts when you tap it, so you can begin anywhere — at Evita's lane if that's your priority — and pick up the rest in any order.

Which languages is the audio guide in?

English and Spanish. You choose the language in the app; each person in your party can listen in their own.

Is it suitable for children?

Better than a group tour: you set the pace, skip stops, sit in the shade and leave when you like. The stories are 3–5 minutes each, and the cemetery itself — cats, angels, a boxer, a caretaker with a broom — holds children's attention surprisingly well. Remind them it's an active cemetery.

How much does it cost, and for how many people?

€9.99 per person, one payment, access for a year. Each traveller needs their own purchase and phone; there is no subscription.
